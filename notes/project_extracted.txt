1‐ Project Information

Project Title: ZeroWatt Wind

Project Objective:

  i. Design and prototype a passive, wind‐driven mechanical system requiring no electricity

  ii. Capture ambient wind energy using a Fibonacci‐spiral turbine, optimized for low, incon‐
      sistent urban wind speeds

 iii. Transmit that rotational energy via a gear drivetrain from the point of capture to the point
      of need

 iv. Provide a reliable, low‐cost alternative to electric HVAC and aerators during power outages
     and in dense urban or low‐income areas


Participant Organisation:

Both participants are studying Mechanical Engineering at IUT and have a special interest in fluid
mechanics.

The Project’s Contribution to the City:

Application 1: Passive Wind Power Harnessing Air Ventilator

The Urban Problem: To keep outdoor pollution and rising city heat out, new buildings are built
airtight. Thick insulated walls, sealed windows and no natural airflow gaps. While that is good
when the purpose is to keep the bad air out, it also means the building can no longer breathe
on its own. Every bit of fresh air and cooling now has to come from electric HVAC systems,
which during load shedding, cannot function.

Reason why Passive Ventilation can’t compensate: Cities have traditionally used passive ven‐
tilation (such as windows and basic thermal buoyancy vents) but these are largely insufficient
in high density urban environments, being too weak to overcome the internal heat loads gen‐
erated by people and electronics.

Affects on people: Stagnant air leads to poor indoor air quality, making occupants sick (known
as Sick Building Syndrome). CO2 builds up as exhaled air isn’t cleared out. Harvard’s COGfx
study found a 500 ppm CO2 rise cut cognitive performance by 15%, with higher PM2.5 also
slowing response times and accuracy.

Solution: Our designed system directly targets this issue. Wind speed increases significantly
with height due to reduced surface friction, which is why a Fibonacci turbine is intentionally
mounted at rooftop level to maximize wind energy capture. It drives the motion through a
two‐stage bevel gear system, redirecting rotation 90◦ to walk the motion down through the
wall or ceiling cavity to a zone that would otherwise see little to no natural airflow. It is in the
upper cavity where the system intercepts the natural phenomenon of thermal buoyancy. In any
enclosed space, heat generated by occupants and electronics naturally rises and pools at the
ceiling. While passive vents are often too weak to clear this heavy heat load, our system actively
drives NACA 65‐810 aerodynamically efficient fan blades to aggressively force this buoyant hot
air out. By actively exhausting this upper heat layer, it creates a negative pressure that draws
fresh, cooler air into the lower occupant zones As a result, we get a fully passive system with
no failure during load shedding and no added carbon footprint.

The moving air also continuously carries humidity out and replaces it with drier air, which pre‐
vents the damp conditions that cause mould growth and wood rot.

Application 2: Pond Aerator to increase the amount of Dissolved Oxygen

Problem: In a rapidly developing city, urban water bodies face a silently growing ecological
threat, that is stagnation. Surrounded by concrete, these bodies of water are cut off from
natural currents. Without continuous movement, they lose dissolved oxygen, which leads to
toxic algae blooms, foul odors and ideal breeding grounds for mosquitoes. This, in turn, disrupts
aquatic habitats.

Traditional Approach: Traditional answer to it is dropping electrically powered aerators into the
water. But running underwater high‐voltage electrical cables across public parks is dangerous,
expensive to install and creates a permanent drain on the city’s power grid.

Solution: The same mechanism we designed solves this very problem efficiently. Our system
uses a surface paddle wheel. From an engineering standpoint, this is the most efficient way to
increase dissolved oxygen (DO). The rotating paddles aggressively break the surface tension of
the water, physically splashing and churning it. This maximizes the surface area of the water
exposed to the air, accelerating natural gas exchange, while simultaneously destroying the still‐
water surface tension. All while it is powered by wind, instead of electricity.

Unlike a constantly running electric aerator, our system doesn’t need to run continuously to be
effective. Pond stagnation develops due to thermal stratification where warm surface water
sits on cooler, oxygen‐poor water below. Intermittent aeration, triggered naturally by gusts of
wind, is enough to repeatedly disrupt this layering before it stabilizes. In long periods without
wind, the pond behaves similarly to any water body with limited wind access but the wind
conditions in most urban open spaces are rarely still for extended stretches and even brief
aeration events meaningfully slow the rate of oxygen depletion.

Innovation offered by the Project:

Our turbine uses a fibonacci (logarithmic spiral) blade arrangement, where blade pitch and
spacing follow the golden‐ratio progression found throughout nature (nautilus shells, sun‐
flower seed heads, pinecones). Some of its features are as follows:

  i. Progressive pitch: each blade meets the wind at a gradually changing angle along its spiral
     path, which smooths airflow entry and reduces the abrupt separation that causes turbu‐
     lence and drag in flat bladed rotors.

  ii. Self‐starting at low wind speeds: the asymmetric spacing means at any given moment,
      several blades are positioned at different effective angles of attack, improving the odds
      that at least one blade catches enough torque to initiate rotation even in light or gusty
      urban wind, a known weak point of a ventilator known as whirlybirds.

 iii. Reduced vibration and noise: It creates quieter, more consistent rotation, which matters
      for a device meant to sit above occupied rooms or public parks.

For the fan’s blade part, we moved away from flat blades entirely and selected the NACA 65‐810
airfoil profile, a blade originally developed for axial‐flow fans and compressors. It’s a proven,
published profile engineered for low‐speed, high‐efficiency air movement. We wrote airfoil
shape equation using scipy in python which helped to create NACA 65‐810 shape more accu‐
rately. Some of the features are:

  i. Lift‐based airflow: Cambered cross‐section moves more air per rotation than a flat blade,

     for the same input torque.

 ii. Compensates for gear losses: Higher aerodynamic efficiency offsets torque lost through
     the two‐stage bevel gear drivetrain.

 iii. Low turbulence: Minimizes flow separation at low fan speeds, producing a steady breeze

 iv. Quieter operation: Smoother airflow transition reduces the noise typically caused by tur‐
